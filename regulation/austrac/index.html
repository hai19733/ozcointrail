AUSTRAC Registration: What It Means & How to Check in 2026

Last verified: 1 October 2026 · Written by James Walker · Source reviewed against AUSTRAC, ASIC and exchange documentation

AUSTRAC registration is one of the most misunderstood labels in Australian crypto. It means a provider is registered under Australia’s anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing regime. It is not the same as an Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL), it is not a government recommendation, and by itself it does not guarantee that customer funds will be protected if a platform fails.

The important question is therefore not simply whether an exchange says it is “regulated”. You need to know which entity is registered, what services that registration covers, and whether separate ASIC licensing applies to the product you are using.

The short answer

Registration ≠ licence — and you can check it yourself

Virtual asset service providers that provide covered services in Australia generally need to be registered with AUSTRAC. That registration primarily relates to Australia’s AML/CTF framework, and it requires providers to meet obligations covering customer identification, transaction monitoring, reporting, record keeping and other controls designed to reduce money-laundering and terrorism-financing risk.

An AFSL, by contrast, is administered by ASIC and authorises specified financial services or products. The two should not be treated as interchangeable.

The simplest way to think about it is this: AUSTRAC registration tells you that the provider is registered to provide covered virtual-asset services under the AML/CTF framework. An AFSL tells you that an entity has ASIC authorisation for particular financial services. Neither label means the Australian government recommends the platform.

What AUSTRAC registration actually is

AUSTRAC is Australia’s financial-intelligence and AML/CTF regulator.

Crypto businesses exchanging digital currency for fiat have been subject to AUSTRAC registration requirements since 2018. Australia expanded the framework in 2026, replacing the narrower digital currency exchange terminology with a broader virtual asset service provider, or VASP, regime.

Covered services now extend beyond simple crypto-to-fiat exchange and can include services such as:

  • exchanging virtual assets for money;
  • exchanging one virtual asset for another;
  • virtual-asset safekeeping;
  • transferring virtual assets on behalf of customers; and
  • certain financial services connected with the offer or sale of virtual assets.

Providers offering registrable virtual-asset services with the required Australian connection generally need to enrol and register with AUSTRAC before providing those services.

Registration also has to be maintained. AUSTRAC can impose conditions and may refuse, suspend, cancel or refuse to renew a registration in appropriate circumstances.

What obligations come with registration?

AUSTRAC registration is not simply a badge placed in a website footer. Depending on the services provided and the rules that apply, a registered provider can have obligations covering areas such as:

Customer identification

Platforms need procedures for identifying customers and performing customer due diligence. This is why Australian exchanges ask users to complete KYC checks rather than allowing unrestricted anonymous trading.

Transaction monitoring

Providers need systems and controls designed to identify unusual activity and manage money-laundering and terrorism-financing risks.

Suspicious matter reporting

Transactions or behaviour that meet the relevant legal threshold may need to be reported to AUSTRAC.

Threshold transaction reporting

Certain reportable transactions, including relevant cash transactions of A$10,000 or more, can trigger reporting requirements.

Record keeping

Providers must keep specified customer, transaction and compliance records for the periods required by law.

Travel Rule obligations

Australia’s 2026 AML/CTF reforms also expanded Travel Rule requirements for virtual-asset transfers. For newly regulated virtual-asset services, relevant Travel Rule obligations began applying from 1 July 2026. In broad terms, the rule requires regulated institutions involved in covered transfers to collect or transmit specified information about the parties to the transaction.

These are serious compliance obligations. But their purpose is primarily to detect and prevent financial crime — not to insure your crypto balance against platform failure.

Registration is not a licence

This is the distinction that matters most. The words registered, licensed and regulated are often used interchangeably in marketing, but legally they can describe very different things.

An Australian Financial Services Licence is issued under the financial-services regime administered by ASIC. An AFSL contains specific authorisations, and holding one licence does not automatically mean every activity performed by the company is covered by that licence.

For example, an exchange may:

  • provide spot crypto trading through one entity;
  • provide derivatives through another entity;
  • offer a payment product under a separate licence or authorised-representative arrangement.

That means you should check the product and legal entity, rather than simply seeing “AFSL” somewhere on the company’s website and assuming it covers everything.

Registration is also not a government endorsement

AUSTRAC registration should not be described as an Australian government approval of an exchange. AUSTRAC itself assesses whether businesses fall within and comply with its AML/CTF framework.

Registration does not mean AUSTRAC has concluded that:

  • the exchange is a good investment platform;
  • its fees are competitive;
  • its custody practices are risk-free;
  • it will remain solvent;
  • its crypto assets are insured; or
  • customers are guaranteed repayment if the business collapses.

The same caution applies to an AFSL: a financial-services licence is a regulatory authorisation, not an endorsement or investment recommendation.

How to check AUSTRAC registration yourself

Australia now has a public Virtual Asset Service Provider Register, making this much easier than it used to be. You do not need to rely on screenshots or claims made by an exchange.

Step 1: Find the legal entity

Do not start with the logo. Look at the exchange’s Australian Terms of Use, Product Disclosure Statement, Financial Services Guide, website footer or regulatory disclosure page. Find the Australian company name and, ideally, its ABN or ACN. A global crypto brand can operate through several different companies.

Step 2: Open AUSTRAC’s VASP Register

Go to AUSTRAC’s website and open the Virtual Asset Service Provider Register. The public register became available in 2026 and allows users to check whether a provider is registered.

Step 3: Search the company

AUSTRAC allows searches using identifiers such as legal or trading name, ABN, ACN or ARBN. Using the legal entity or registration number is generally safer than relying only on the brand.

Step 4: Match the entity

Confirm that the company appearing in the register is actually the company providing the service you intend to use. Do not assume that the registration of one subsidiary automatically covers every international entity using the same brand.

Step 5: Check the registration details

Review the information displayed by AUSTRAC, including any available registration details or conditions. AUSTRAC can refuse, suspend, cancel or refuse to renew registrations where the legal requirements are met.

Step 6: Check ASIC separately

If the company claims to hold an AFSL, search ASIC’s professional registers separately. Then determine which legal entity holds the AFSL, the AFSL number, and whether the service or product you are interested in appears to fall within that entity’s authorisations. This second check is important because AUSTRAC registration and ASIC licensing are separate regulatory questions.

Examples from major Australian crypto platforms

The regulatory structure can become much clearer when you look at actual entities.

Binance Australia

Binance Australia’s Australian services are provided through Investbybit Pty Ltd. Binance Australia documentation identifies Investbybit as registered with AUSTRAC as a virtual-asset services provider. Its April 2026 Binance Pay documentation stated that no AFSL had yet been assigned and that an AFSL application was to be lodged. Because Australia’s licensing transition continued during 2026, users checking its status today should verify the current position directly against Binance’s latest Australian disclosures and ASIC’s registers rather than relying on an older document.

OKX Australia

Spot crypto exchange services for Australian customers are provided by OKX Australia Pty Ltd. Derivatives and margin products are provided separately by OKX Australia Financial Pty Ltd, which states that it holds AFSL 379035. That is a useful example of why the entity matters: the company providing spot trading is not necessarily the same company issuing a regulated financial product. See OKX’s Australia help page for its own entity breakdown.

CoinSpot

CoinSpot has operated as an AUSTRAC-registered crypto provider in Australia. Its payments-related financial services involve Casey Block Services Pty Ltd, which holds AFSL 562554. That AFSL should not automatically be interpreted as meaning every CoinSpot spot-crypto activity is provided under the licence.

Swyftx

Swyftx Pty Ltd is AUSTRAC registered and also holds AFSL 568543. Swyftx itself states that its spot cryptocurrency exchange services are not provided under that AFSL. Again, seeing an AFSL number beside an exchange name is not enough — you need to understand what the licence actually covers.

Why “regulated exchange” can be misleading

Imagine two websites both say “we are a regulated Australian crypto exchange.” That sentence alone tells you very little. One might mean registered with AUSTRAC for AML/CTF purposes; another might mean registered with AUSTRAC plus an associated company has an AFSL for derivatives; a third could mean an AFSL applies only to a payment card or another financial product. All three could use the word “regulated”, while the customer protections and licensing arrangements are very different.

A better question is: registered or licensed by whom, through which entity, and for which service?

What changed in 2026?

2026 has been an important year for Australian crypto regulation.

1. AUSTRAC’s public VASP Register launched

AUSTRAC made its Virtual Asset Service Provider Register publicly available on 30 June 2026. Consumers can now check a provider’s registration directly rather than relying solely on claims made by the platform.

2. The VASP regime became broader

Australia expanded the AML/CTF framework beyond the old digital-currency-exchange model. Covered virtual-asset services can now include crypto-to-crypto exchange, custody and certain transfer-related activities, in addition to fiat-to-crypto exchange.

3. Travel Rule obligations expanded

Relevant obligations for newly regulated virtual-asset services began applying from 1 July 2026. Providers therefore need systems capable of handling specified information associated with covered virtual-asset transfers.

4. AUSTRAC increased enforcement and register clean-up

AUSTRAC has been actively reviewing inactive and higher-risk registrations. On 7 September 2026, AUSTRAC said it had cancelled, suspended or refused renewal for 45 remittance and virtual-asset service provider registrations during the previous year. A company appearing on a register should therefore still be checked for its current status and legal entity.

5. Australia passed a broader digital-asset licensing framework

The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 passed Parliament on 1 April 2026 and received Royal Assent on 8 April 2026. The new regime is scheduled to commence on 9 April 2027, with ASIC responsible for licensing and supervising digital asset platforms falling within the regime.

That makes one point particularly important: AUSTRAC registration will continue to matter for AML/CTF compliance, but it should not be confused with the financial-services licensing framework that applies alongside it.

Common claims that get AUSTRAC registration wrong

  • “AUSTRAC registered means regulated like a bank.” No. Banks operate under a substantially different prudential and financial-services regulatory framework. AUSTRAC registration primarily concerns AML/CTF obligations.
  • “AUSTRAC registration protects my funds.” Not by itself. Registration does not create a general crypto deposit-insurance scheme or guarantee repayment if an exchange fails.
  • “If a company has an AFSL, all of its crypto products are licensed.” Not necessarily. AFSL authorisations are specific, and crypto brands frequently operate through multiple legal entities. Check the entity and product.
  • “Every crypto exchange Australians can access is AUSTRAC registered.” Accessibility is not the same as legal registration. Check the public register rather than assuming that a website available from Australia has the required status.
  • “AUSTRAC registered means the government recommends the exchange.” No. Registration is a regulatory status, not a government recommendation.

A better five-minute exchange check

Before sending a significant amount of money to an Australian crypto platform, check these five things:

  1. Legal entity — who are you actually contracting with?
  2. AUSTRAC registration — does that entity appear on the VASP Register?
  3. ASIC status — does the company or a related entity claim to hold an AFSL, and what exactly does that licence cover?
  4. Custody terms — who legally holds the crypto and fiat deposited on the platform?
  5. Product-specific protections — do derivatives, cards, staking, lending or other products use a different company or regulatory structure?

This tells you much more than a generic “regulated exchange” badge.

Frequently asked questions

What does AUSTRAC registration actually mean?

It means the provider is registered under Australia’s AML/CTF framework to provide covered virtual-asset services and must comply with applicable obligations. It does not by itself mean the provider holds an AFSL.

Is AUSTRAC registration the same as a licence?

No. AUSTRAC registration and an ASIC Australian Financial Services Licence are separate regulatory concepts. Registration relates to AML/CTF compliance, while an AFSL authorises specified financial services.

How do I check if a crypto platform is AUSTRAC registered?

Use AUSTRAC’s public Virtual Asset Service Provider Register. Search using the provider’s legal or trading name, or identifiers such as its ABN or ACN, and match the registered entity to the website you are using.

Does AUSTRAC registration protect my funds?

Not in the way bank deposit protection does. Registration does not create a general guarantee, insurance scheme or promise that crypto assets will be returned if the provider fails.

Does an AFSL mean an exchange’s spot crypto service is licensed?

Not automatically. AFSL authorisations are specific, and crypto brands frequently operate through multiple legal entities. Check which entity holds the licence and which financial services that licence authorises.

Is every crypto exchange operating in Australia AUSTRAC registered?

Providers of registrable virtual-asset services with the required connection to Australia are required to register. That does not mean every crypto website accessible from Australia is necessarily compliant — check the AUSTRAC register directly.
