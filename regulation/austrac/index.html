AUSTRAC Registration: What It Means & How to Check in 2026

Last verified: 1 October 2026 · Written by James Walker · Source reviewed against AUSTRAC, ASIC and exchange documentation

AUSTRAC registration is the most misunderstood label in Australian crypto. It shows a platform has anti-money-laundering controls in place — nothing more. It is not a licence, not a government endorsement, and not a promise that your funds are protected. Here is what it actually means, what it does and doesn’t cover, and how to check a platform’s registration yourself in a few minutes.

The short answer

Registration ≠ licence — and it’s easy to check

AUSTRAC registration means a crypto platform is enrolled in Australia’s anti-money-laundering (AML/CTF) regime. It does not mean ASIC has licensed it, it does not mean the government endorses it, and it does not bring the consumer protections that attach to an AFSL. You can verify any provider in minutes using AUSTRAC’s public register — the trick is to search the legal entity name, not the brand name.

At a glance: AUSTRAC registration vs an AFSL

Aspect AUSTRAC registration AFSL (ASIC licence)
Issued by AUSTRAC ASIC
What it covers Anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing (AML/CTF) compliance Specified financial products and services
What it means for you The platform must verify your ID, monitor transactions and report suspicious activity Additional consumer protections may apply, depending on the product
Where to check AUSTRAC public register ASIC registers

In short, the two labels answer different questions: AUSTRAC registration answers who is allowed to operate, while an AFSL answers what they can sell you and how you’re protected.

What AUSTRAC registration actually is

AUSTRAC is Australia’s financial-intelligence regulator, and it administers the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006. Since 2018, businesses that exchange digital currency — buying and selling crypto for cash or for other crypto — have been required to register with AUSTRAC as digital currency exchanges. In 2026 AUSTRAC opened its public register of virtual asset service providers (VASPs), so consumers and businesses can look up a provider directly rather than taking a platform’s “we’re registered” badge at face value.

Registration is not a one-off form. It obliges the provider to meet ongoing AML/CTF requirements, including:

  • Identity verification (KYC) — confirming who you are before you trade.
  • Suspicious matter reporting — reporting transactions that show signs of money laundering or other serious crime.
  • Threshold transaction reporting — reporting cash transactions of A$10,000 or more.
  • Record keeping — holding transaction and identity records, typically for several years.

These are anti-money-laundering duties aimed at detecting crime. They are not the same thing as looking after your money.

Registration is not a licence, and not an endorsement

The single biggest source of confusion is that “registered” gets blurred with “licensed” — and both get blurred with “endorsed.” They are three different things.

An Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) is issued by ASIC and authorises specified financial services, such as dealing in regulated financial products. Depending on the product, that licensing regime can bring additional obligations and consumer protections. AUSTRAC registration is a separate AML/CTF enrolment, and it should not be treated as equivalent to an AFSL.

Equally, holding either an AUSTRAC registration or an AFSL is not a government endorsement of the business or its products. A platform that says it is “AUSTRAC registered” is stating a compliance fact — not that any regulator recommends it, and not that your funds are safe.

Read that distinction twice

“Registered” answers who is allowed to operate (AUSTRAC, anti-money-laundering). “Licensed” answers what they can sell you and how you’re protected (ASIC, financial services). Neither word means “the government vouches for this platform.” A lot of marketing copy collapses all three into one word: “regulated.”

What it protects — and what it doesn’t

The cleanest way to see the limits of AUSTRAC registration is to put its coverage side by side with what it does not give you.

AUSTRAC registration covers It does not cover
Identity verification (KYC) Segregation of your funds from the platform’s own funds
Suspicious-matter and threshold reporting Insurance or a compensation scheme if the platform fails
Ongoing customer due diligence and record keeping AFCA dispute resolution (this depends on the product or entity)
Monitoring for signs of money laundering A guarantee that the platform is solvent or well-run

The practical takeaway: AUSTRAC registration is about stopping crime, not about protecting your trading balance. If something goes wrong with a platform, registration alone does not get your money back.

How to check a platform’s registration yourself

Rather than trusting a footer badge, you can verify a platform in a few minutes. The whole process comes down to matching the right entity name.

  1. Go to AUSTRAC’s public register on the AUSTRAC website (austrac.gov.au). It is searchable by the provider’s legal entity name or registration number.
  2. Search the legal entity name, not the brand. This is the step most people miss. Binance Australia, for example, trades through Investbybit Pty Ltd; OKX Australia’s spot exchange runs through OKX Australia Pty Ltd. The brand on the website is not necessarily the name on the register.
  3. Match the entity to the website. Confirm the registered entity is the one actually operating the site you are using. A global brand may operate a separate, unregistered product that looks identical.
  4. Check the status. Look for whether the registration is registered and active, or whether it has been suspended or cancelled. AUSTRAC has warned that some businesses stay listed even after they stop operating, so a listing alone is not proof the platform is currently active.

If you find the entity, it matches the site, and the status is active, you have done more due diligence than most people do. If any of those checks fails, that is a signal to pause before sending money.

What changed in 2026

Australia’s crypto rules are moving, and a few 2026 changes reframe what “AUSTRAC registered” means in practice.

  • A public VASP register. AUSTRAC opened its register of virtual asset service providers to the public, giving consumers a direct way to check registration status.
  • The Travel Rule. Expanded Travel Rule obligations for covered providers took effect on 1 July 2026, requiring originator and beneficiary information to accompany transfers between regulated providers. See our crypto regulation guide for the fuller picture.
  • A new licensing regime is coming. The Digital Assets Framework received Royal Assent on 8 April 2026 and brings digital asset platforms into a financial-services licensing regime, with its main provisions commencing in 2027.
  • Clean-up of inactive and non-compliant providers. AUSTRAC has reported cancelling, suspending or refusing to renew registrations for a large number of crypto and remittance providers over the past year, and has warned that inactive exchanges risk deregistration.

Net effect: registration is becoming easier to check at the same time as it is becoming a baseline that fewer platforms can rely on alone. From 2027, the AFSL will matter more — but that does not change what AUSTRAC registration has always been: an AML/CTF enrolment, not a guarantee.

Claims that get it wrong

When you read other sites on this topic, a few statements crop up that don’t hold up.

  • “AUSTRAC registered = regulated like a bank.” Wrong. It is an AML/CTF enrolment, not a prudential or consumer-protection regime.
  • “AUSTRAC registered means your funds are protected.” Wrong. Registration does not segregate funds, insure balances or pay compensation if a platform fails.
  • “Every exchange you see is AUSTRAC registered.” Wrong. Registration is required, but not everyone complies, and AUSTRAC has suspended and cancelled registrations.
  • “AUSTRAC registered = the government recommends it.” Wrong. Registration is a compliance status, not an endorsement of the business or its products.

Frequently asked questions

What does AUSTRAC registration actually mean?

It means the platform is enrolled in Australia’s anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing (AML/CTF) regime, which requires it to verify customer identities, monitor transactions and report suspicious activity. It does not mean ASIC has licensed the business, and it is not a government endorsement.

Is AUSTRAC registration the same as a licence?

No. AUSTRAC registration is an AML/CTF enrolment. An Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) is issued by ASIC and authorises specified financial services. They cover different things, and holding one does not mean holding the other.

How do I check if a platform is AUSTRAC registered?

Search AUSTRAC’s public register on austrac.gov.au using the provider’s legal entity name rather than its brand name — for example, Binance Australia trades through Investbybit Pty Ltd. Then confirm the registered entity matches the website you are using, and check the registration status is active rather than suspended or cancelled.

Does AUSTRAC registration protect my funds?

Not directly. AUSTRAC registration is about anti-money-laundering compliance, not consumer protection. It does not guarantee segregated client funds, insurance, or compensation if the platform fails. Those protections, where they exist, come from separate arrangements such as an AFSL or a product’s own terms.

Is every crypto exchange operating in Australia AUSTRAC registered?

They are required to be, but not all are. AUSTRAC can suspend, cancel or refuse registration, and it has publicly warned that some businesses remain listed while no longer operating. This is why checking the current status yourself, rather than trusting a marketing badge, matters.
