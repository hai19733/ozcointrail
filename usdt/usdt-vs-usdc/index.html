USDT vs USDC: Which Is Safer & Which to Use in Australia

Last verified: 5 October 2026 · By James Walker · Editor, OzCoinTrail

USDT and USDC are both US-dollar stablecoins designed to stay close to US$1, but they are not interchangeable in terms of liquidity, reserves, regulation or network support.

The short answer for Australians:

  • Choose USDT if your main goal is trading, moving funds between exchanges or accessing the widest range of crypto pairs.
  • Choose USDC if your priority is reserve transparency, regulatory oversight and holding a dollar-denominated balance rather than actively trading.
  • Neither is the same as holding AUD or USD in an insured bank account.

USDT is currently the larger and more liquid stablecoin, with around US$184 billion in circulation, while USDC has around US$72 billion. USDC, however, publishes reserve information more frequently and its reserves are concentrated mainly in cash and short-dated US government debt.

For Australians, there is another factor that is easy to overlook: USDT and USDC are crypto assets for tax purposes, not simply digital cash. Your result in AUD can therefore change because of the AUD/USD exchange rate even if the stablecoin itself remains close to US$1.

Decision: USDT wins on liquidity and practical exchange use. USDC wins on reserve disclosure and regulatory structure. That does not mean one is universally "safe" and the other is "unsafe" — they carry different types of risk.

What are USDT and USDC?

USDT and USDC are fiat-backed stablecoins: blockchain tokens whose issuers hold reserve assets and aim to keep each token worth approximately US$1. They perform broadly the same function — allowing users to hold and transfer something that behaves like a digital US dollar without using a traditional bank transfer every time. The difference is the company and reserve structure behind each token.

What is USDT?

USDT is issued by Tether. It is the largest stablecoin by circulation and is used extensively as a quote currency on cryptocurrency exchanges. You will commonly see trading pairs such as BTC/USDT, ETH/USDT, SOL/USDT and BNB/USDT. That wide exchange support is the main reason USDT remains the default stablecoin for active crypto trading.

What is USDC?

USDC is issued by Circle, a US-based financial technology company that listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 2025. USDC competes less on sheer trading volume and more on reserve transparency, regulated financial infrastructure, institutional use, and Ethereum and Solana ecosystem integration.

In simple terms: USDT behaves more like the crypto market's default trading dollar, while USDC is positioned more like a regulated digital-dollar infrastructure product.

Is USDC safer than USDT?

There is no single definition of "safer". If by safety you mean reserve transparency and regulatory oversight, USDC currently has the stronger profile. If by safety you mean liquidity, exchange acceptance and the ability to exit a position quickly, USDT has the advantage. The correct comparison therefore depends on the type of risk you care about.

USDT vs USDC reserves and transparency

The first question behind any stablecoin is simple: is there enough high-quality backing to support the US$1 peg?

USDC's reserves are held primarily in cash and short-term US Treasury assets, including assets held through the Circle Reserve Fund, a government money-market fund managed by BlackRock. Tether's reserves are more diversified — in addition to US Treasury holdings and cash-equivalent assets, its disclosures have included assets such as gold, Bitcoin, secured loans and other investments. That does not automatically make USDT unsafe, but it means users are taking exposure to a broader reserve mix.

An attestation is not the same as an audit. Neither USDT nor USDC had published a completed full financial audit as of the latest verification date for this article. An attestation checks information at a particular point in time, while a full financial audit generally involves a broader review of financial statements, systems, controls and liabilities. That distinction matters when comparing stablecoin reserve claims.

USDC's 2023 depeg shows why "transparent" does not mean risk-free

USDC provides stronger reserve transparency, but that did not prevent it from temporarily losing its US$1 peg in March 2023. At the time, around US$3.3 billion of Circle's reserves were held at Silicon Valley Bank when the bank failed. USDC briefly traded below US$1 before recovering.

This is an important example because it shows that even high-quality reserves can face banking risk, liquidity risk, redemption pressure and market panic. Reserve transparency reduces uncertainty. It does not eliminate stablecoin risk.

Which is more regulated: USDT or USDC?

USDC currently has the broader traditional regulatory footprint. Circle operates through US financial licensing structures and has regulatory relationships including US state money-transmitter licences and New York financial regulation. USDC is also structured to operate under the European Union's MiCA regime. Tether operates under a different regulatory model and has established a framework in El Salvador. This difference matters most to users who care about institutional adoption and formal regulatory oversight. However, regulation does not automatically guarantee that a stablecoin can never lose its peg.

What does this mean for Australians?

For Australian users, there are two separate questions: how is the stablecoin issuer regulated, and how is the exchange you use regulated in Australia? These are not the same thing. Holding USDC does not automatically mean the exchange where you bought it is safer, and holding USDT through an Australian-facing exchange does not mean Tether itself is regulated in Australia.

Australia's digital-asset regulatory framework is still developing, with broader licensing changes expected to take greater effect from 2027. For most Australian users today, you should therefore check:

  • whether the exchange operates legally in Australia,
  • whether it is registered with AUSTRAC where required,
  • how customer assets are held,
  • how withdrawals work,
  • what fees apply,
  • whether your chosen network is supported.

The most important transfer rule: the sending and receiving network must match. If you withdraw USDT using TRC-20, the receiving wallet or exchange must support USDT on Tron. Do not assume that because an address accepts USDT, it accepts USDT on every blockchain. A wrong-network transfer can be difficult or impossible to recover. Is USDT cheaper to transfer than USDC?

Not necessarily. The transfer fee depends more on the blockchain network and the exchange's withdrawal fee than on whether you are using USDT or USDC. USDT on Tron is often inexpensive, while USDT on Ethereum may cost more; USDC on Solana can be very inexpensive, while USDC on Ethereum can become more expensive when network activity rises.

So the better question is not "Is USDT or USDC cheaper?" — it is "Which supported network gives me the lowest total cost without creating compatibility risk?"

Choose USDT if you mainly trade crypto

For active traders, USDT is usually the more practical choice — not because it is automatically safer, but because the crypto market is still heavily denominated in USDT. That gives it more trading pairs, deeper order books, tighter spreads on many exchanges, and easier exchange-to-exchange movement. If you regularly move between Bitcoin, Ethereum, altcoins and stablecoins, USDT often creates the least friction.

Choose USDC if you mainly want to hold a dollar balance

For users who are not actively trading, USDC may be more attractive. Its main advantages are reserves concentrated in cash and short-term US government debt, more frequent reserve disclosure, and stronger traditional regulatory infrastructure. That makes USDC a reasonable choice when the main goal is temporarily parking funds in a US-dollar-denominated crypto asset — though it still should not be treated as equivalent to an insured bank deposit.

Should Australians hold USDT or USDC long term?

Holding either stablecoin for long periods creates risks that do not exist when holding insured Australian-dollar deposits — issuer risk, reserve risk, banking-counterparty risk, blockchain risk, wallet risk, exchange risk, regulatory risk and US-dollar exchange-rate risk against AUD. If you are simply waiting a short period before another crypto trade, the convenience of a stablecoin may make sense. If the goal is long-term capital preservation rather than crypto trading, it is worth asking whether you actually need blockchain exposure at all.

AUD/USD risk matters even when the stablecoin stays at US$1

Australian users often focus only on whether USDT or USDC remains at US$1, but your actual economic result is normally measured in Australian dollars. Suppose you buy US$1,000 worth of USDC: even if USDC remains exactly at US$1, the AUD value of your position can rise or fall because the Australian dollar moves against the US dollar. That is separate from stablecoin depeg risk. You are effectively exposed to two things: the stablecoin remaining near US$1, and the USD/AUD exchange rate.

USDT and USDC tax in Australia

Both USDT and USDC are treated as crypto assets for Australian tax purposes, so a disposal may create a capital gains tax event. Examples can include selling USDT for AUD, selling USDC for AUD, swapping USDT for Bitcoin, swapping USDC for Ethereum, exchanging USDT for USDC, or spending stablecoins on goods or services. Simply purchasing and continuing to hold a stablecoin generally does not itself create a disposal event. However, because the value is calculated in AUD, changes in the AUD/USD exchange rate can contribute to a taxable capital gain or loss. See our crypto tax in Australia guide for the full picture.

USDT vs USDC: final verdict

For most Australians, the decision is straightforward: use USDT when liquidity and trading access matter most, and use USDC when reserve transparency and regulatory structure matter most. USDT's biggest advantage is its dominance across cryptocurrency exchanges — it is easier to trade, easier to transfer between many platforms and supported across more markets. USDC's biggest advantage is the structure behind the coin: more frequent reserve disclosures and reserves concentrated largely in cash and short-dated government debt. Neither eliminates risk, and neither should be treated exactly like money in an Australian bank account. Whichever stablecoin you choose, the exchange, transfer network and AUD conversion cost can matter just as much as the stablecoin itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is USDC safer than USDT?

On reserve disclosure and regulatory structure, USDC has the stronger profile. Circle publishes reserve information more frequently and holds reserves mainly in cash and short-dated US Treasuries. USDT is significantly larger and more liquid, but Tether reports its reserve composition less frequently and uses a broader mix of reserve assets. Neither stablecoin is an insured bank deposit.

Which is bigger, USDT or USDC?

USDT is considerably larger. At the latest verification date for this article, USDT had around US$184 billion in circulation compared with roughly US$72 billion for USDC. That scale contributes to USDT's deeper trading liquidity.

Which is better for trading in Australia?

For active crypto trading, USDT is usually better because more exchanges and cryptocurrencies have USDT trading pairs. The exchange fee still matters, so compare the exact AUD funding and trading route before depositing money.

Which is better for holding?

If your priority is reserve transparency rather than trading liquidity, USDC is generally the stronger choice. However, neither USDC nor USDT should be treated as equivalent to an insured savings account.

Can USDT lose its peg?

Yes. Any stablecoin can trade above or below US$1 temporarily if market demand, redemption confidence or reserve concerns change. USDT has experienced periods of price stress in the past, although it has generally returned toward its target price.

Can USDC lose its peg?

Yes. USDC fell below US$1 in March 2023 after Circle disclosed that US$3.3 billion of reserves was held at Silicon Valley Bank. It later recovered after the banking situation was resolved.

Is USDT backed by real dollars?

USDT is backed by a portfolio of reserve assets rather than only physical US dollars in bank accounts. Those reserves include US Treasury securities and cash-equivalent assets, along with other categories such as gold, Bitcoin and secured loans.

Is USDC backed 1:1?

Circle structures USDC so that reserves equal or exceed the amount of USDC in circulation. Those reserves are held primarily in cash and short-dated US Treasury assets. That does not make USDC risk-free, because those reserve assets and banking relationships still involve operational and counterparty risk.

Which network is cheapest for USDT?

TRC-20 on Tron is commonly used for lower-cost USDT transfers, but the cheapest option depends on the exchange's withdrawal fee. Always check the fee shown on the withdrawal screen before sending.

Which network is cheapest for USDC?

Low-cost networks such as Solana and Base can make USDC transfers inexpensive. Ethereum is more widely supported but may cost more during periods of high network activity.

Can I swap USDT to USDC in Australia?

Yes. Many exchanges allow USDT and USDC to be traded directly or converted through another market. The conversion may create a taxable disposal for Australian tax purposes, so keep transaction records.

Are USDT and USDC taxable in Australia?

Yes. Both are crypto assets for Australian tax purposes. Selling, swapping or spending them can create a capital gains tax event. Your result is calculated in Australian dollars, so movements in AUD/USD can affect the capital gain or loss even if the stablecoin remains close to US$1.

Is USDT better than USDC?

Neither is universally better. USDT is better for liquidity and trading; USDC is better for reserve transparency and regulatory structure. The best choice depends on what you intend to do after buying it.

Using USDT to trade?

If you're holding USDT to trade rather than to park funds, Binance's 0.10% spot fee and deeper order books are usually the cheaper home for it.
