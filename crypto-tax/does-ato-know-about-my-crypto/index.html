Does the ATO Know About My Crypto?

Last verified: 3 October 2026 · Written by James Walker · Source reviewed against current ATO guidance

Short answer: yes — if you've used a crypto exchange or other crypto service provider operating in Australia, you should assume the ATO may already have information about your account and transactions.

The ATO runs a crypto-assets data-matching program that obtains identity and transaction data from crypto service providers and compares it with Australian tax returns. The current published protocol covers financial years 2014–15 through to 2025–26.

That does not mean the ATO can automatically see every private wallet transaction you have ever made. But if your activity touches an identified exchange account, Australian bank account or other regulated service, there may already be enough information to connect that activity back to you.

Here is what the ATO can collect, how the matching works, what happens with Binance and other exchanges, and what to do if your past tax returns are incomplete.

The short answer

The ATO has operated a crypto-assets data-matching program for several years.

Its current published protocol covers the period from 2014–15 to 2025–26 and is designed to identify people and entities that buy, sell or otherwise transact in crypto assets.

The ATO estimates that records relating to roughly 700,000 to 1.2 million individuals and entities may be obtained for each financial year covered by the program.

The important point is this:

If you have used an identifiable crypto account, do not assume the ATO only knows about your crypto because you declared it yourself.

The ATO can obtain third-party data and compare it with your tax return.

Where the information does not line up, that data can be used for education, risk profiling and compliance activity.

For the tax treatment itself, see our crypto tax in Australia guide.

How does the ATO know you own crypto?

The main mechanism is data matching.

The ATO obtains data from selected crypto-asset designated service providers and compares that information against records it already holds.

That may allow it to connect:

  • your identity;
  • your exchange account;
  • linked bank details;
  • wallet addresses associated with that account;
  • and individual crypto transactions.

The ATO's published program says the information is used to identify buyers and sellers of crypto assets and quantify relevant transactions.

This is different from the idea that the ATO simply watches every blockchain address in Australia.

Instead, the strongest link is often created when a regulated account connects a real identity to transactions, bank accounts or wallet addresses.

What information can the ATO collect?

The ATO's crypto data-matching protocol covers considerably more than a person's name and the fact they opened an exchange account.

Depending on the information held by the provider, collected fields can include:

CategoryExamples of information that may be collected
Identity informationName, date of birth, residential address, ABN where applicable, email address and phone number
Account informationUser or account ID, account type, account status, sign-up date and IP-address information
Verification informationDetails associated with identity verification and account identification
Banking informationBank account details linked to the crypto account
Wallet informationWallet addresses associated with the account
Transaction informationTransaction date and time, crypto asset type, fiat amount, crypto amount, transfer type and transaction description
Account-level informationAccount balances and certain unique account identifiers

So the ATO may receive enough information to identify both who owns an account and what occurred inside that account.

That still does not mean every item of data above is supplied by every provider in every case. The exact records depend on the provider and the information available to it.

Does the ATO know which crypto exchanges I use?

Potentially, yes.

However, there is an important distinction that many crypto-tax articles miss:

the ATO does not publish a complete list of the crypto providers selected for its data-matching program.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner has allowed the ATO to withhold the names of participating providers from the published protocol.

That means it is safer not to rely on online lists claiming that a particular exchange is either definitely “reported” or definitely “not reported”.

If a crypto business operates in Australia and falls within the relevant regulatory and data-collection framework, you should not assume that using it keeps your activity outside the ATO's reach.

Does CoinSpot report to the ATO?

The ATO does not publicly confirm CoinSpot by name as a provider selected under the current crypto-assets data-matching protocol.

CoinSpot is an Australian crypto platform operating within Australia's regulated crypto environment, so users should not assume their CoinSpot activity is invisible to the ATO.

For tax purposes, the practical approach is to keep and report your records correctly regardless of whether a provider appears on an unofficial online list.

Does Binance report to the ATO?

The same principle applies to Binance.

The ATO does not publish a provider-by-provider list showing which exchanges have supplied data under its crypto data-matching protocol.

Accordingly, it is too strong to say that the ATO has publicly confirmed Binance as a named reporting provider.

But Australian users should not assume Binance activity is invisible to the ATO.

Depending on the services used, relevant information may be identifiable through regulated entities, banking records, transaction records and other information available to Australian authorities.

If you use Binance, keep complete account and transaction exports for your own tax records.

Can the ATO see my crypto wallet?

This depends on what you mean by “see”.

Exchange wallets

If the wallet is part of a custodial exchange account linked to your identity, the exchange may hold information connecting you to that account and its activity.

That type of information can potentially fall within the ATO's data-matching framework.

Self-custody wallets

A self-custody wallet does not normally contain your name or tax file number on the blockchain.

Simply looking at a blockchain address therefore does not necessarily tell the ATO who owns it.

But a wallet can become attributable to you when it interacts with something that already knows your identity — for example:

  • you withdraw from a verified exchange to that wallet;
  • you deposit from that wallet into a verified exchange;
  • the address is recorded against an exchange account;
  • or other financial and transaction records establish the connection.

So self-custody is not the same thing as anonymity.

What about crypto-to-crypto swaps?

This is one of the most important tax rules Australian crypto users miss.

You do not need to convert crypto back into Australian dollars before a tax event can occur.

When you exchange one crypto asset for another, you are generally disposing of the asset you gave up.

For example:

BTC → USDT

can involve a CGT event for the BTC.

So can:

ETH → SOL

or another crypto-to-crypto exchange.

You generally need to work out the Australian-dollar market value of the crypto received at the time of the transaction and use the relevant value when calculating the gain or loss.

That means someone who traded between coins for several years but never withdrew AUD may still have a substantial disposal history that needs to be considered for tax.

Our crypto tax guide explains disposals and capital gains in more detail.

Does simply buying crypto create a tax bill?

Not necessarily.

Buying crypto with Australian dollars and continuing to hold it is generally different from disposing of it.

The tax issue usually arises when a relevant disposal or income event occurs.

Common examples include:

  • selling crypto for AUD;
  • exchanging one crypto asset for another;
  • using crypto to acquire goods or services where the relevant tax rules apply;
  • gifting or otherwise disposing of crypto;
  • receiving certain staking rewards or other crypto income.

Moving crypto between wallets you own is generally not itself a disposal merely because the coins changed addresses, although transaction fees and the exact circumstances still need to be considered.

What makes the ATO take a closer look?

Data matching does not automatically mean you have done anything wrong.

Its purpose is to compare third-party information with the information already held by the ATO.

A mismatch may attract attention where, for example:

  • exchange records show crypto disposals that do not appear to have been reported;
  • reported figures are inconsistent with the available transaction history;
  • income-related crypto activity appears to be missing;
  • or other financial records do not align with what was lodged.

An exchange record also does not necessarily contain the entire tax story.

For example, a platform may know that crypto left your account but not whether you:

  • transferred it to another wallet you owned;
  • paid someone;
  • moved it to another exchange;
  • or disposed of it.

That is one reason your own records remain important.

Can the ATO see offshore crypto exchanges?

Using an overseas exchange does not automatically make your crypto invisible.

The ATO's existing domestic crypto data-matching program is only one source of information.

Depending on the circumstances, information may also be connected through:

  • Australian bank transfers;
  • transactions involving Australian-regulated service providers;
  • international information-sharing arrangements;
  • records obtained in an investigation;
  • and identifiable transfers between exchanges and wallets.

The international reporting framework is also becoming broader.

CARF will expand international crypto reporting

Australia has committed to implementing the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework — CARF.

CARF is designed specifically for automatic exchange of tax information relating to crypto assets between participating countries.

Under Australia's announced implementation timetable, the framework and a parallel domestic crypto reporting regime are expected to commence in 2027, with the first domestic reporting to the ATO and first exchanges of information with foreign tax authorities expected in 2028.

In practical terms, CARF is intended to make cross-border crypto holdings and transactions easier for tax authorities to identify.

So relying on an overseas exchange simply because it is outside Australia is likely to become an increasingly weak assumption.

What about the crypto Travel Rule?

The Travel Rule is separate from the ATO's tax data-matching program.

It comes from Australia's anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing regime administered by AUSTRAC, not the ATO.

Under the reformed rules, virtual asset service providers can be required to collect, verify and pass certain payer and payee information when transferring virtual assets.

For relevant virtual-asset services, expanded obligations took effect during 2026.

There is no general minimum transaction amount below which the Travel Rule automatically disappears.

Transfers involving self-hosted wallets also have specific requirements: although information may not always need to be transmitted to another institution, a provider can still have obligations to collect and verify information relating to the transfer.

The Travel Rule does not itself determine whether you owe tax. It is an AML/CTF identification and information-sharing requirement.

What should I do if I forgot to report crypto?

Do not start by guessing a number.

First reconstruct the records.

Ideally, collect:

  1. exchange transaction exports;
  2. deposit and withdrawal records;
  3. wallet addresses you controlled;
  4. bank deposits and withdrawals;
  5. records from exchanges you no longer use;
  6. staking, reward and other income records;
  7. acquisition dates and original cost information.

Then identify which transactions were:

  • purchases;
  • disposals;
  • wallet-to-wallet transfers;
  • income;
  • fees;
  • or transfers between your own accounts.

If a previous return is incorrect, you may be able to amend the return.

Depending on the circumstances, a voluntary disclosure may also be relevant. Coming forward before compliance action begins can affect how penalties are treated.

There is not one universal “28-day crypto rule” applying to every ATO data-matching case. If the ATO contacts you, use the response deadline shown in the actual notice or correspondence.

If the records span several financial years, involve large amounts, or you are unsure how the transactions should be classified, consider speaking with a registered tax agent.

How far back can the ATO's crypto data go?

The ATO's current published crypto-assets data-matching protocol covers financial years:

2014–15 through to 2025–26.

That is important because the program is not limited to people who started trading recently.

The ATO has also stated that data obtained under this program may be retained for seven years from receipt of the final instalment of verified data files, reflecting the long holding periods that can occur with crypto assets and the need for longer-term analysis.

What records should crypto investors keep?

At minimum, keep records that let you establish:

  • the date of each transaction;
  • what crypto asset was involved;
  • the quantity;
  • the Australian-dollar value at the time;
  • what you paid to acquire it;
  • what you received when disposing of it;
  • relevant fees;
  • the purpose of the transaction;
  • and the other wallet or account involved where relevant.

Keeping only your current exchange balance is not enough.

A tax calculation may depend on transactions that occurred years before the eventual sale.

If you move assets between exchanges, retaining the withdrawal record from one side and the matching deposit record on the other side can also help demonstrate that a transaction was a transfer rather than a disposal.

Frequently asked questions

Does the ATO know I have crypto?

If you have used a crypto exchange or service provider connected to Australia's regulated financial system, you should assume the ATO may already have information about your account or activity. The ATO's crypto-assets data-matching program obtains identity and transaction data from selected crypto service providers and matches it against tax records.

Does the ATO know if I use Binance?

The ATO does not publish a complete list of providers selected for its current crypto data-matching program, so it does not officially provide a public Binance yes/no list. Australian Binance users should nevertheless not assume their activity is invisible to the ATO.

Does CoinSpot report to the ATO?

The ATO deliberately does not publish the names of all designated service providers participating in the crypto data-matching program. CoinSpot users should therefore keep complete records and meet their tax obligations rather than relying on claims that a particular exchange does or does not report.

Is swapping crypto for another coin taxable?

A crypto-to-crypto exchange is generally a disposal for CGT purposes. For example, exchanging BTC for USDT can create a CGT event for the BTC even though you never receive Australian dollars.

Does transferring crypto between my own wallets create CGT?

Generally, moving the same crypto between wallets you own is not itself a disposal because ownership has not changed. However, transaction fees and other circumstances can have separate tax consequences, so retain records showing both sides of the transfer.

Can the ATO see MetaMask or another self-custody wallet?

A blockchain address does not automatically identify its owner. However, if that address is linked to a verified exchange account or other identifiable records, authorities may be able to connect the wallet to you.

Can the ATO see offshore crypto?

Potentially. International exchange of information, banking records and connections between identified exchange accounts and wallets can all provide information. Australia's planned implementation of CARF is expected to increase automatic international crypto tax reporting from 2027, with the first reporting and information exchanges expected in 2028.

What happens if I never reported my crypto?

If an earlier tax return is incorrect, you may need to amend it. Reconstruct your transactions before making corrections and consider a registered tax agent where multiple years or significant amounts are involved. If the ATO has already contacted you, follow the response deadline stated in the actual correspondence rather than assuming there is a standard crypto-specific deadline.

How long does the ATO keep crypto data?

Under the current crypto-assets data-matching protocol, data can be retained for approximately seven years from receipt of the final instalment of verified data files.
