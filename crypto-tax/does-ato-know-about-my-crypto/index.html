Does the ATO Know About My Crypto?

Last verified: 3 October 2026 · Written by James Walker · Source reviewed against current ATO guidance

Short answer: yes — if you've used a crypto exchange or other identifiable crypto service, you should assume the ATO may already have information about your account and transactions.

The ATO runs a crypto-assets data-matching program that obtains identity and transaction information from selected crypto service providers and compares it with Australian tax records. Its current published protocol covers financial years 2014–15 through to 2025–26.

That does not mean the ATO can automatically identify every self-custody wallet or see every blockchain transaction as belonging to you. But once a wallet, transaction or bank transfer can be connected to an identifiable exchange account, there may already be enough information to link the activity back to you.

Here is what the ATO can obtain, how crypto data matching works, whether exchanges such as Binance and CoinSpot are publicly confirmed, and what to do if your previous tax returns are incomplete.

The short answer

The ATO has operated a crypto-assets data-matching program for years.

Its current published protocol covers 2014–15 to 2025–26 and is intended to identify taxpayers who buy, sell or otherwise dispose of crypto assets.

The ATO estimates that data relating to approximately 700,000 to 1.2 million individuals and entities may be obtained for each financial year covered by the program. It also says it uses more than 60 identity-matching techniques when matching third-party data to taxpayers.

The practical takeaway is simple:

If you have used an identifiable crypto account, do not assume the ATO only knows about your crypto because you reported it yourself.

Third-party records can be compared against information already held by the ATO, including your lodged tax returns.

For the actual tax treatment of gains, losses and income, see our crypto tax in Australia guide.

How does the ATO know you own crypto?

The main mechanism is data matching.

The ATO obtains records from selected crypto-asset designated service providers and matches that information against its own systems.

Depending on the records available, this can help connect:

  • your identity;
  • your exchange account;
  • linked bank details;
  • wallet addresses recorded against the account;
  • and individual crypto transactions.

The ATO says the program is used to identify people who may have failed to report crypto disposals and to support education, risk identification and compliance activity.

This is different from saying the ATO automatically knows the owner of every public blockchain address.

In many cases, the important link is created when a regulated or verified service connects a real-world identity with an account, wallet address or transaction history.

What information can the ATO collect?

The ATO's crypto data-matching program can involve considerably more information than simply your name and the fact that you opened an exchange account.

Depending on what the provider holds, the records can include:

CategoryExamples of information that may be collected
Identity informationName, date of birth, residential address, ABN where relevant, email and phone number
Account informationUser or account ID, account type, status, sign-up information and IP-address information
Verification informationInformation associated with identifying and verifying the account holder
Banking informationBank account details associated with the account
Wallet informationCrypto wallet addresses linked to the account
Transaction informationDates, asset type, fiat amount, crypto amount, transfer type and transaction description
Account-level informationAccount identifiers, balances and related account records

So the information may be enough to establish both who controls an account and what transactions occurred through it.

That does not mean every provider supplies every field in every case. The data available depends on the provider and the records it holds.

Does the ATO know which crypto exchanges I use?

Potentially.

But there is an important distinction that many articles overlook:

the ATO does not publish the complete list of crypto providers participating in its current data-matching program.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner granted the ATO an exemption allowing it to withhold the names of the designated service providers involved.

That means you should be cautious with online lists claiming:

  • “Exchange X definitely reports to the ATO”; or
  • “Exchange Y does not report to the ATO.”

Unless there is separate official confirmation, the public ATO protocol itself does not provide that provider-by-provider list.

Does CoinSpot report to the ATO?

The ATO does not publicly identify CoinSpot by name in its current crypto-assets data-matching protocol.

That distinction matters.

It would therefore be too strong to say that the ATO's published protocol officially confirms that CoinSpot is one of its selected providers.

However, CoinSpot users should not interpret the absence of a public provider list as meaning their activity is invisible to Australian tax authorities.

The safer approach is to keep complete transaction records and report your tax position correctly regardless of whether a platform appears on a third-party list.

Does Binance report to the ATO?

The same issue applies to Binance.

The ATO's current public protocol does not provide a provider-by-provider list confirming Binance as a selected data source. The OAIC has specifically permitted the ATO not to publish those provider names.

So rather than saying “Binance definitely reports every Australian account to the ATO”, the more accurate conclusion is:

Australian Binance users should not assume their Binance activity is invisible to the ATO.

Relevant information may potentially be connected through exchange records, Australian banking activity, regulated service providers and other information available to authorities.

If you use Binance, retain complete exports of your transaction and transfer history for your own tax records.

Can the ATO see my crypto wallet?

It depends on what type of wallet you mean.

Exchange wallets

If your wallet forms part of a custodial exchange account that has been verified in your name, the provider may hold records connecting that account and its transactions to your identity.

Information of that kind can potentially be used in third-party reporting or data-matching processes.

Self-custody wallets

A self-custody wallet such as MetaMask does not normally display your name, address or tax file number on the blockchain.

Looking at a blockchain address alone therefore does not necessarily tell the ATO who owns it.

However, the address may become attributable to you if other records establish the connection.

For example:

  • you withdraw from a verified exchange to the wallet;
  • you later send assets from the wallet back to a verified exchange;
  • the address appears in records held by a service provider;
  • or financial and transaction records otherwise connect the address to you.

So self-custody should not be treated as the same thing as anonymity.

Are crypto-to-crypto swaps taxable?

Generally, yes — and this is one of the most commonly misunderstood parts of Australian crypto tax.

You do not have to convert crypto into Australian dollars before a disposal can occur.

The ATO states that disposing of crypto can include exchanging one crypto asset for another.

For example:

BTC → USDT

can be a disposal of the BTC.

So can:

ETH → SOL

or another crypto-to-crypto trade.

You generally need to determine the relevant Australian-dollar market value when calculating the capital gain or loss.

This means a person could trade between crypto assets for several years without withdrawing any AUD and still build up a substantial history of CGT events.

Does buying crypto create a tax bill?

Not by itself in the ordinary case.

Buying crypto with Australian dollars and continuing to hold that asset is different from disposing of it.

Common events that may have tax consequences include:

  • selling crypto for AUD;
  • swapping one crypto asset for another;
  • using crypto to buy goods or services where the relevant tax rules apply;
  • gifting or otherwise disposing of crypto;
  • and receiving certain staking rewards or other crypto income.

A transfer of the same asset between wallets you control is generally different from transferring ownership to someone else.

However, fees and the exact facts of the transaction can still matter, so keep records of wallet-to-wallet transfers.

What makes the ATO take a closer look?

Data matching does not itself mean you have done anything wrong.

Its purpose is to compare information from different sources and identify cases that may need clarification or correction.

Examples of potential mismatches include:

  • exchange data indicating disposals that do not appear in a tax return;
  • transaction history that does not align with the amounts reported;
  • crypto income that appears to be missing;
  • or other financial records that conflict with the lodged return.

The ATO specifically says matched data can be used to identify taxpayers who may have failed to report a crypto disposal.

But an exchange record may not tell the whole story.

For example, an exchange may know that crypto left your account without necessarily knowing whether you:

  • transferred it to another wallet you own;
  • moved it to another exchange;
  • paid another person;
  • or otherwise disposed of it.

That is why keeping your own transfer records remains important.

Can the ATO see offshore crypto exchanges?

Using an overseas exchange does not automatically make your crypto invisible.

The ATO's domestic crypto data-matching program is only one possible source of information.

Depending on the circumstances, crypto activity may also be connected through:

  • Australian bank deposits and withdrawals;
  • Australian-regulated crypto or payment providers;
  • exchange and wallet records;
  • international information-sharing arrangements;
  • and information obtained through compliance or investigation activity.

International tax reporting around crypto is also expanding.

What is CARF?

CARF is the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework.

It is designed to create a standardised international system under which crypto service providers report information that can then be exchanged between participating tax authorities.

Australia has publicly committed to implementing CARF, and Treasury has consulted on how the framework should be incorporated into Australian law.

Australia also joined an international statement committing participating jurisdictions to work towards activating exchange arrangements in time for CARF information exchanges to commence by 2027, subject to each country's domestic legislative processes.

That is important wording.

It should not be treated as meaning every Australian CARF obligation is already in force or that every offshore exchange is already automatically reporting Australian users under CARF.

The precise Australian reporting rules and implementation details depend on domestic legislation.

But the direction is clear: cross-border crypto tax information is becoming more standardised and easier for participating tax authorities to exchange.

What about the crypto Travel Rule?

The Travel Rule is separate from ATO tax reporting.

It forms part of Australia's anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing regime administered by AUSTRAC.

For relevant transfers, businesses such as virtual asset service providers may need to collect, verify and pass information about the parties involved in a transfer.

For new registrable virtual-asset services, certain AML/CTF obligations — including relevant Travel Rule obligations — applied from 1 July 2026 under the transitional arrangements.

AUSTRAC also states that there is no general minimum transfer amount below which the Travel Rule automatically stops applying.

What about self-hosted wallets?

There are specific rules for transfers involving self-hosted wallets.

For example, when a reporting institution transfers virtual assets to a self-hosted wallet, it may not have to send Travel Rule information to another institution, because there may be no other institution in the transfer chain.

However, it can still have obligations to collect and verify relevant payer and payee information.

The Travel Rule does not decide whether you owe tax.

It is an AML/CTF identification and information requirement, while ATO crypto tax and data matching are separate systems.

What should I do if I forgot to report crypto?

Start with your records — not with an estimate.

Try to reconstruct:

  1. exchange transaction exports;
  2. deposit and withdrawal records;
  3. wallet addresses you controlled;
  4. Australian bank deposits and withdrawals;
  5. records from exchanges you no longer use;
  6. staking, reward and other crypto income;
  7. purchase dates and cost-base information.

Then separate transactions into categories such as:

  • purchases;
  • disposals;
  • wallet-to-wallet transfers;
  • income;
  • fees;
  • and movements between your own accounts.

If an earlier tax return is incorrect, you may need to amend it.

Depending on the circumstances, a voluntary disclosure may also be relevant.

Do not assume there is a universal “28-day crypto rule” applying to every ATO data-matching case. If the ATO contacts you, use the deadline shown in the actual notice or correspondence.

For several years of transactions, large amounts or complicated wallet histories, consider using a registered tax agent rather than estimating the result.

How far back can the ATO's crypto data go?

The current published crypto-assets data-matching protocol covers:

2014–15 through to 2025–26.

That means the program is not limited to people who only recently started buying crypto.

The ATO's protocol also provides for crypto data to be retained for an extended period because crypto assets may be held for many years before disposal.

This is another reason old transaction records can still matter.

What records should crypto investors keep?

At minimum, your records should allow you to establish:

  • the transaction date;
  • which crypto asset was involved;
  • the quantity;
  • its relevant Australian-dollar value;
  • what you originally paid for it;
  • what you received on disposal;
  • applicable fees;
  • the purpose of the transaction;
  • and the wallets or accounts involved where relevant.

Keeping only your current exchange balance is not enough.

A capital gain calculated today may depend on an acquisition that happened several years ago.

If you move crypto between exchanges or wallets you own, retain records from both sides of the transfer.

That can help demonstrate that an outgoing transaction from one platform was a transfer to another account you controlled rather than a disposal to another person.

Frequently asked questions

Does the ATO know I have crypto?

It may. The ATO operates a crypto-assets data-matching program that obtains account and transaction information from selected crypto service providers and compares that data with Australian tax records. If you use identifiable crypto services, you should not assume the ATO only knows about crypto transactions you voluntarily tell it about.

Does the ATO know if I use Binance?

The ATO does not publicly disclose the complete list of providers selected for its current crypto data-matching program. That means its public protocol does not provide an official Binance yes/no list. Australian Binance users should nevertheless not assume Binance activity is automatically outside the ATO's reach.

Does CoinSpot report to the ATO?

The ATO does not publicly name every provider involved in the crypto data-matching program. The OAIC has specifically allowed the ATO to withhold those provider names. CoinSpot users should therefore maintain complete records and meet their tax obligations rather than relying on unofficial lists of “reporting” and “non-reporting” exchanges.

Is swapping crypto for another coin taxable?

Generally, the swap involves a disposal of the crypto you give up. The ATO expressly includes exchanging one crypto asset for another as a type of crypto disposal. For example, BTC → USDT may trigger a CGT event for the BTC even if you never receive Australian dollars.

Does transferring crypto between my own wallets create CGT?

A genuine transfer between wallets you own is different from disposing of an asset to someone else because beneficial ownership has not changed. Keep records showing both sides of the transaction so you can demonstrate that it was a transfer.

Can the ATO see MetaMask or another self-custody wallet?

A public blockchain address does not automatically identify its owner. But if a wallet address can be linked to an identified exchange account, bank record or other information, authorities may potentially connect it to you.

Can the ATO see offshore crypto?

Potentially. Using an offshore platform does not automatically remove your activity from the ATO's reach. Banking records, identifiable exchange activity, international information exchange and connections between known accounts and wallets can all be relevant. CARF is also intended to expand international exchange of crypto tax information as participating jurisdictions implement the framework.

What happens if I never reported my crypto?

If an earlier return is incorrect, you may need to amend it. First reconstruct your transactions so that you know which activities were purchases, disposals, income or transfers between your own wallets. If the amounts are significant or several financial years are involved, consider speaking with a registered tax agent.

How long does the ATO keep crypto data?

The current crypto-assets data-matching protocol allows relevant data to be retained for an extended period to support matching, compliance activity and situations where crypto assets may be held for several years before disposal. Check the current ATO protocol if the exact retention period is material to your circumstances.
