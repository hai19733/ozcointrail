Does the ATO Know About My Crypto?

Last verified: 3 October 2026 · Written by James Walker · Source reviewed against current ATO guidance

Short answer: yes — if you've bought, sold or swapped crypto through an Australian exchange or another “designated service provider”, the ATO likely already holds your account and transaction data. You don't have to report anything yourself first. The ATO's crypto-assets data-matching program pulls records from platforms each year, matches them against tax returns, and flags people who left crypto disposals out. Here's exactly how it works, what it collects, which platforms report, and what to do if you think you've under-reported.

The short answer

The ATO has run a crypto-assets data-matching program for years, and its published protocol covers financial years 2014–15 through to 2025–26. Each year it obtains account and transaction records on an estimated 700,000 to 1.2 million individuals and entities, then matches those records against lodged returns. If you've used an Australian exchange, the realistic answer to “does the ATO know?” is almost always yes — the question is really whether your return matches what it already has.

This matters because crypto disposals — including swaps between coins — are capital gains tax (CGT) events. If the ATO's data shows a disposal you didn't report, you can expect it to follow up. See our full guide to crypto tax in Australia for how gains and losses are actually calculated.

How the ATO's crypto data-matching works

The program obtains data from crypto “designated service providers” — in practice, the AUSTRAC-registered exchanges and payment businesses operating in Australia. The ATO negotiates with each provider individually, so the exact list and the exact fields aren't publicly confirmed for every platform, but the mechanism is well documented:

  • Legal authority: the ATO collects the data under section 353-10 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953, and the provider is legally obliged to supply it.
  • How often: data is obtained annually, typically between April and July, covering the preceding period.
  • Who it covers: an estimated 700,000–1,200,000 individuals and entities each financial year.
  • How it's matched: the ATO uses more than 60 identity-matching techniques to link third-party data to taxpayer records.
  • How long it's kept: each year's data is retained for around 7 years from receipt of the final verified data files.

The program protocol is submitted to the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, which is the privacy oversight layer on top of the tax-collection purpose.

What the ATO collects about you

The data falls into two broad groups: who you are, and what you did. Both are more extensive than most people expect.

CategoryWhat the ATO collects
Identity detailsName, date of birth, address, ABN, email, phone number and social media profiles
Account detailsUser ID, account type, sign-up date, registration IP address and identity-verification records
Transaction dataDates and times, crypto asset type, amounts in fiat and crypto, and transfer type and description
Funding linksLinked bank accounts and wallet addresses, plus account balances

In short, the ATO isn't just told “you bought some Bitcoin” — it can receive the dates, amounts and counterparties behind your activity, which is enough to reconstruct a disposal history.

Crypto-to-crypto swaps: the gap most people miss

If there's one thing the data-matching program is designed to catch, it's the crypto-to-crypto swap. Many people assume tax only applies when they cash out to Australian dollars — but swapping Bitcoin for USDT, or USDT for another coin, is a disposal and therefore a CGT event, even though no AUD ever touched your account.

Because exchanges record every one of those swaps, a trader who converted between coins repeatedly can trigger a chain of taxable events they never reported. The ATO's matching doesn't need you to confess anything; the disposal history is already in the data it receives. Our crypto tax guide walks through what counts as a disposal in detail.

What makes the ATO take a closer look

Data matching isn't a penalty by itself — it's a comparison. The ATO is most likely to act when what it holds doesn't line up with what you declared:

  • Unreported disposals, especially crypto-to-crypto swaps, that appear in exchange data but not in your return.
  • Income you omitted, such as staking rewards or airdrops that a platform reported.
  • Bank records that don't match — large deposits or withdrawals that suggest buying or selling you didn't declare.
  • Values that look wrong relative to the transaction history the ATO already has.

What to do if you've under-reported

If you think a past return left crypto out, the ATO's process gives you room to fix it before things escalate. When its matched data suggests a discrepancy, it will generally give you at least 28 days to verify or correct the information before taking administrative action.

Your options, roughly in order of preference:

  • Lodge an amendment to correct an earlier return.
  • Make a voluntary disclosure, which can reduce the penalties that would otherwise apply — penalties are generally lower (or waived) when you come forward before the ATO contacts you.
  • Get your records straight first — export your exchange history before you calculate anything, since platforms can restrict access to older data.

If you're unsure how far back to correct or how to value the assets, this is the point to talk to a registered tax agent rather than guess.

What's coming next: CARF

The current program mostly reaches Australian-registered platforms. The next layer is the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF), which Australia is preparing to implement — it extends crypto reporting across borders on the same model as the existing Common Reporting Standard for bank accounts. In practice, that means offshore exchanges become progressively less of a hiding place, and the data the ATO holds about your crypto will only get more complete. This sits alongside the AML/CTF Travel Rule, which already requires virtual asset service providers to collect and pass on payer and payee information for relevant transfers — a separate AUSTRAC obligation covered in our AUSTRAC registration guide, but part of the same trend toward greater identification around crypto.

Frequently asked questions

Does the ATO know if I buy crypto on Binance?

Yes, in most cases. Binance (including its Australian entity) is repeatedly named in connection with the ATO's crypto data-matching program, and the ATO states it obtains data from crypto exchanges operating in Australia. If you bought through Binance Australia, the ATO can receive your account and transaction details without you reporting them first.

Does CoinSpot report to the ATO?

Yes. CoinSpot is an AUSTRAC-registered digital currency exchange and is named as one of the Australian platforms whose data reaches the ATO under the crypto data-matching program.

Is swapping crypto for another coin taxable?

Yes. A crypto-to-crypto swap, including into a stablecoin such as USDT, is a disposal for capital gains tax purposes. You may realise a capital gain or loss on the coin you swapped out, even if you never converted it back to Australian dollars.

What happens if I never reported my crypto?

If the ATO's matched data suggests you left crypto disposals out of a return, it will generally give you at least 28 days to verify or correct the information before taking administrative action. You can also lodge an amendment or make a voluntary disclosure, which can reduce penalties.

Can the ATO see my offshore crypto?

Offshore accounts are harder for the ATO to see directly, but not invisible. The ATO can obtain data through international information-sharing arrangements (such as the Common Reporting Standard), blockchain analytics, and your bank records showing transfers to and from overseas exchanges. The OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) will extend this further.

How long does the ATO keep my crypto data?

The ATO retains each financial year's crypto data for around seven years from receipt of the final verified data files, in line with its record-keeping practices.
